Obtain the simple logical expression of the following. Draw the corresponding switching circuit.} \vspace{0.4cm} \noindent \textbf{(iv)} $(p \wedge q \wedge \sim p) \vee (\sim p \wedge q \wedge r) \vee (p \wedge \sim q \wedge r) \vee (p \wedge q \wedge r)$ \hfill \textcolor{red}{\textbf{(4 Marks)}} \vspace{0.6cm} \noindent \textbf{Solution:} \vspace{0.3cm} \noindent \textbf{Step 1: Assigning variables to the switches.} [1 Mark] \vspace{0.2cm} \noindent Let $p$: The switch $S_1$ is closed. \\ Let $q$: The switch $S_2$ is closed. \\ Let $r$: The switch $S_3$ is closed. \\ Let $\sim p$: The switch $S_1'$ is closed. \\ Let $\sim q$: The switch $S_2'$ is closed. \\ Let $L$: The lamp L is on. \vspace{0.6cm} \noindent \textbf{Step 2: Logical Simplification using Laws of Logic.} [2 Marks] \vspace{0.2cm} \noindent \textit{\textcolor{blue}{(ONLY FOR Explanation: We first look for obviously contradicting terms. Notice the first bracket has both $p$ and $\sim p$ connected by AND. This will immediately become False. Let's start there.)}} \vspace{0.2cm} \noindent We simplify the given expression: \begin{align*} & (p \wedge q \wedge \sim p) \vee (\sim p \wedge q \wedge r) \vee (p \wedge \sim q \wedge r) \vee (p \wedge q \wedge r) \\ & \equiv (p \wedge \sim p \wedge q) \vee (\sim p \wedge q \wedge r) \vee (p \wedge \sim q \wedge r) \vee (p \wedge q \wedge r) && \text{(Commutative Law)} \\ \intertext{\textit{\textcolor{blue}{(ONLY FOR Explanation: $p \wedge \sim p$ is a contradiction, which evaluates to False ($F$).)}}} & \equiv (F \wedge q) \vee (\sim p \wedge q \wedge r) \vee (p \wedge \sim q \wedge r) \vee (p \wedge q \wedge r) && \text{(Complement Law)} \quad \text{\textbf{[1/2 Mark]}}\\ & \equiv F \vee (\sim p \wedge q \wedge r) \vee (p \wedge \sim q \wedge r) \vee (p \wedge q \wedge r) && \text{(Identity Law)} \\ & \equiv (\sim p \wedge q \wedge r) \vee (p \wedge \sim q \wedge r) \vee (p \wedge q \wedge r) && \text{(Identity Law)} \\ \intertext{\textit{\textcolor{blue}{(ONLY FOR Explanation: Now observe the last two brackets: $(p \wedge \sim q \wedge r)$ and $(p \wedge q \wedge r)$. Both contain $p$ and $r$. We can factor out $(p \wedge r)$ using the Distributive Law backwards.)}}} & \equiv (\sim p \wedge q \wedge r) \vee [ (p \wedge r) \wedge (\sim q \vee q) ] && \text{(Distributive Law)} \quad \text{\textbf{[1/2 Mark]}}\\ & \equiv (\sim p \wedge q \wedge r) \vee [ (p \wedge r) \wedge T ] && \text{(Complement Law)} \\ & \equiv (\sim p \wedge q \wedge r) \vee (p \wedge r) && \text{(Identity Law)} \\ \intertext{\textit{\textcolor{blue}{(ONLY FOR Explanation: Both remaining parts now share the term $r$. Let's pull $r$ out as a common factor to the right.)}}} & \equiv [ (\sim p \wedge q) \vee p ] \wedge r && \text{(Distributive Law)} \\ \intertext{\textit{\textcolor{blue}{(ONLY FOR Explanation: Apply the Distributive Law again to open the inner bracket: $p$ distributes over $\sim p$ and $q$. This is a crucial step!)}}} & \equiv [ (p \vee \sim p) \wedge (p \vee q) ] \wedge r && \text{(Distributive \& Commutative Law)} \quad \text{\textbf{[1/2 Mark]}}\\ & \equiv [ T \wedge (p \vee q) ] \wedge r && \text{(Complement Law)} \\ & \equiv (p \vee q) \wedge r && \text{(Identity Law)} \quad \text{\textbf{[1/2 Mark]}} \end{align*} \vspace{0.4cm} \noindent \textbf{Step 3: Drawing the corresponding switching circuit.} [1 Mark] \vspace{0.2cm} \noindent \textit{\textcolor{blue}{(ONLY FOR Explanation: The final simplified expression is $(p \vee q) \wedge r$. The $\vee$ (OR) means $p$ ($S_1$) and $q$ ($S_2$) are connected in parallel. The $\wedge$ (AND) means this entire parallel combination block is connected in series with $r$ ($S_3$).)}} \vspace{0.5cm} \begin{center} \begin{tikzpicture}[thick, scale=1.1] % Battery \draw (3.5,0) -- (3.5, 0.4); \draw (3.7, -0.2) -- (3.7, 0.6); \node at (3.6, -0.6) {Battery}; \draw (0,0) -- (3.5,0); \draw (3.7,0) -- (5,0); % Lamp \draw (5.5,0) circle (0.4) node {L}; \draw (5,0) -- (5.1,0); \draw (5.9,0) -- (8,0) -- (8,2); % S3 switch (r) in series \draw (8,2) -- (6.5,2); \draw (6.5,2) -- (6.0,2.5); % Open switch angled up \node at (6.2, 2.9) {$S_3$}; \draw (5.8,2) -- (4.5,2); % Parallel split for S1 and S2 \draw (4.5,2) -- (4.5,3) -- (3.5,3); \draw (4.5,2) -- (4.5,1) -- (3.5,1); % S1 switch (p) \draw (3.5,3) -- (2.8,3.5); \node at (3.15, 3.9) {$S_1$}; \draw (2.6,3) -- (1.5,3) -- (1.5,2); % S2 switch (q) \draw (3.5,1) -- (2.8,1.5); \node at (3.15, 1.9) {$S_2$}; \draw (2.6,1) -- (1.5,1) -- (1.5,2); % Closing the loop back to battery \draw (1.5,2) -- (0,2) -- (0,0); % Arrows indicating current flow direction \draw[-latex] (0,1) -- (0,1.1); \draw[-latex] (8,1) -- (8,0.9); \end{tikzpicture} \end{center} \vspace{1cm} \noindent \fbox{ \begin{minipage}{0.97\textwidth} \vspace{0.2cm} \textbf{\large Professor Wadhwa's Note:}\\[0.2cm] \textbf{The "Hidden Absorption" Trap!} Students often panic when they see four long terms spanning an entire equation. The master strategy is systematic neutralization! First, instantly kill the obvious contradiction like $(p \wedge \sim p \rightarrow F)$. Second, become a "bracket hunter." Notice how the giant block $(p \wedge \sim q \wedge r) \vee (p \wedge q \wedge r)$ beautifully folded down into just $(p \wedge r)$? If you miss grouping these properly, you will get stuck in an endless, confusing loop of expansions. Finally, never forget the golden shortcut step: $p \vee (\sim p \wedge q) \equiv p \vee q$. Board examiners purposely design these questions relying on that specific algebraic collapse to filter out the average students from the 100-scorers! \vspace{0.2cm} \end{minipage} } \end{document}
